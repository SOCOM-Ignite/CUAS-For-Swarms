Lognormal blast radius — sizing for confidence

Your blast radius is random, so "big enough" is a probability. Because ln R ~ N(μ, σ²), the median radius you must design to is the required radius inflated by eσ·zp.

Payload & packing

The round is a sphere of diameter D loaded with gumballs, so only the packing fraction φ of its volume is gum — the rest is interstitial void. Mass is derived, not entered: m = φ·(π/6)D³·ρgum + mcasing, and the gumball count is N = φ·(D/dball)³. Presets are the standard regimes: simple cubic 0.524, random loose 0.555, random close packing 0.640 (what you get pouring gumballs into a tube), and ordered FCC/HCP 0.7405 (the theoretical maximum, which you will not hit by pouring). This coupling is the point — growing D raises payload as D³ but frontal area only as D², so with the casing ignored the drag group falls off as k = 0.75·ρ·Cd / (D·φ·ρgum): bigger and denser-packed rounds are ballistically better even though they are draggier in absolute terms. A fixed casing mass breaks that cleanly at the small end — below roughly 30 mm the casing outweighs the gum and the round behaves like dead weight, so the trend is not monotonic there. Your real ceiling is bore diameter and what the cannon can accelerate, not the physics.

Target break-up — what is and isn't physical here

On first gumball contact the drone sheds 16 fragments and stops flying. Be clear about what that represents. Impact energy has to be reckoned in the drone's frame, not the burst frame: a gumball leaves the shell with ~29 m/s of residual ejection speed, but it also carries the round's ~46 m/s forward velocity and meets a drone closing on it, which puts the actual impact at ~63 m/s — the figure the "Impact speed on drone" tile reports. At 0.95 g that is ~1.9 J (½ · 0.00095 kg · 63² m²/s²), not the 0.4 J an ejection-frame reading would suggest. Still nowhere near the hundreds of joules needed to break an airframe, and still comfortably enough to destroy a spinning propeller and foul a rotor — the conclusion is unchanged, but the number is now the one the page renders. So the modelled event is rotor strike → prop fragments shed → loss of lift → tumbling ballistic fall, not a detonation. The debris speed is a free parameter precisely because it is the one part of this that is a visual choice rather than a derived quantity.

The fall, though, is real physics. Mass is conserved across the break-up: the fragments carry your shed percentage of the airframe between them and the wreck keeps the rest, so the post-kill system weighs exactly what the drone did. Fragment frontal area follows geometric scaling A ∝ m2/3, so a fragment's A/m exceeds the intact drone's by (N/shed)1/3 and debris bleeds speed faster than the airframe it came off; the wreck keeps full airframe area against reduced mass, which is right, because what it shed was light high-area propellers. Gumballs already stuck ride the wreck down, and nothing falls through the floor.

Cd is pinned constant for the wreck and its debris, deliberately — the Morrison correlation describes a smooth sphere, and a tumbling airframe and a shattered prop are emphatically not spheres. The wreck is also sized from its own airframe diameter rather than from rhit: rhit is a statistical hit criterion bundling fuzing and sticking margin, and has no business setting a ballistic coefficient.

Not modelled: autorotation, partial-thrust recovery on the surviving rotors, and any flight-controller reaction — all of which would make a real quadcopter's descent messier and slower than the clean arc drawn here. Break-up energy is reported in the sidebar and flagged when it exceeds what a shattering propeller could plausibly release.

Drag law

In C_d(Re) mode the drag coefficient is the Morrison (2013) smooth-sphere correlation, re-evaluated at every RK4 stage from the instantaneous Reynolds number Re = vD/ν. It reproduces both the subcritical plateau (Cd ≈ 0.4–0.5) and the drag crisis, where Cd collapses past Re ≈ 2.6×10⁵ as the boundary layer trips turbulent. This is not academic for you: a 60 mm round at 60 m/s sits at Re ≈ 2.4×10⁵, right on the shoulder of that collapse, where the real Cd is nearer 0.33 than 0.47. The integrated effect on the miss is smaller than that 30% gap suggests — about 3–6%, since the round spends most of its flight slower and back on the plateau. Caveat worth stating in your writeup: the correlation is for a smooth sphere. Surface roughness moves the crisis to lower Re and softens it, so if your round is textured, measure rather than trust this curve. Gumballs sit at Re ≈ 2×10⁴, safely subcritical, where Cd is flat.

Wind

Drag acts on velocity relative to the air, so wind enters the acceleration and nowhere else. Head/tailwind and updraft are modelled; a crosswind is not, because it would push the round out of the vertical plane the whole solver is built on and make this a 3D problem. Wind is the single most influential environmental term here — a ±10 m/s head/tailwind swings the required blast radius by roughly ±25%, more than any payload tuning you can do.

Shot dispersion

Each Monte Carlo shot perturbs launch elevation by a Gaussian aim error and muzzle velocity by a Gaussian percentage, re-integrates the full trajectory, then draws that shot's own coverage outcome at its own miss distance. The RNG is seeded, so results are reproducible.

A counterintuitive result worth knowing, with the crossover pinned down. Because λ ∝ 1/miss² is convex, scatter is not purely harmful: shots that land closer gain more probability than shots that land wider lose. Reff is the break-even point. With the nominal miss outside Reff, dispersion helps — measured over 6,000 shots per point, expected hit rate rises from 56.06% at zero dispersion to 56.69% at 5 mrad and 59.20% at 20 mrad. Move the nominal miss inside Reff (drop to 22 m range) and the sign flips: 90.74% → 88.19% at 8 mrad → 81.00% at 20 mrad. At realistic dispersion the effect is small either way (under a tenth of a point at 3 mrad), so do not design around it — but do not be surprised when tightening your aim fails to help a system that is already missing wide. Fix the miss first; the scatter is second-order.

Burst & dispersal

The cloud is a thin shell of N discrete gumballs, not a filled sphere, so the drone is not hit by the blast "reaching" it — it is hit when the shell sweeps past and a ball lands. A single ball under quadratic drag obeys v(r) = ve·e−kr and r(t) = ln(1+k·vet)/k with k = 0.75·ρ·Cd/(dball·ρgum), which gives a fuse lead time of t* = (ek·m−1)/(k·ve) to put the shell at the miss distance m exactly at closest approach. Coverage there is λ = N·rhit²/(4m²) and P(hit) = 1 − e−λ (Poisson, balls assumed uniform over the shell). Setting λ = 1 defines the effective blast radius Reff = rhit·√N/2 = (rhit/2)·√φ·(D/dball)1.5 — the radius at which you expect exactly one ball on the target. Because λ ∝ 1/d³ while reach only ∝ d, smaller balls dominate: the ballistic floor here is a 0.3 mm ball, so your real limit is how much gum a ball must carry to foul a rotor, not flight. Shell asymmetry is captured, and falls out for free: each ball is integrated with its own total velocity, so forward-ejected balls — which carry the round's speed on top of their ejection speed — see far higher airspeed and shed it far faster than rearward ones. The cloud genuinely deforms. Not modelled: ball–ball collisions during burst, spin and Magnus lift, and burster lobing. Uniform ejection over the sphere is an idealisation; a real charge produces preferred directions.

The live sim

Not a cartoon — every gumball is integrated individually in 3D with RK4 under the same −g·ŷ − k|v|v law as the round, using the ball's own drag group. Ejection directions come from a Fibonacci sphere, so the spread is even and the replay is bit-identical every run rather than reshuffling on each parameter nudge. Balls inherit the round's velocity at burst on top of their ejection velocity. A ball counts as stuck when its centre passes within rhit of the drone centre, after which it rides along with the drone. Up to 420 balls are simulated; above that the display is a subsample and the HUD says so. Out-of-plane balls are faded by depth, and the aqua circle is the mean shell front measured about the cloud's own centroid — not the burst point, since the cloud keeps the round's forward velocity.

The sim and the λ formula will not agree exactly, and neither is a strict bound on the other. λ treats the encounter as a single instant — a drone meeting an infinitely thin shell of radius m. The sim departs from that in two directions at once. It lets the drone traverse a shell of finite thickness over several milliseconds, which picks up balls the snapshot misses; but since the break-up feature landed, first contact also stops the drone dead and drops it out of the cloud, which cuts off accretion the snapshot implicitly allows. (An earlier version of this note claimed the drone sweeps a tube and that λ is therefore a conservative lower bound. That was written before the target could be downed, and the tube argument no longer survives a drone that stops flying on the first hit.) In practice the sim still tends to run a little above λ at default settings — 2 of 80 against λ = 0.82 — but treat that as one deterministic sample of a stochastic quantity, not as a probability, and not as a bound in either direction.

Model

Wad integrated with RK4 under gravity plus quadratic drag a = −g·ŷ − (½ρCdA/m)·|v|·v, A = πD²/4. The drone flies a straight constant-speed line from its start point to the muzzle, so it stays on the initial line of sight for the whole engagement. Miss distance is the true closest approach between the two paths, found by scanning the integration and refining parabolically. Required blast radius is max(0, CPA − rhit).

Assumptions worth stating in your writeup

No crosswind (head/tailwind and updraft are modelled). No lift/Magnus on the round (it is not spinning). Constant air density. The drone holds a straight constant-speed course and never manoeuvres or evades; it only departs that course once it is hit, at which point it becomes an unpowered falling body. Sphere drag coefficient is treated as constant, though in reality Cd varies with Reynolds number — at these speeds a smooth sphere sits near 0.47 but drops sharply through the drag crisis, so treat Cd as a tunable you should measure. Detonation is assumed to occur exactly at closest approach (a perfect proximity fuse); any fuse delay adds miss distance on top of what this reports.

Why a faster drone is easier to hit

Because the drone closes along your line of sight, its speed does not create a lateral lead error — it only shortens the time of flight, which means less time for drag and gravity to pull the wad off the sight line. Closing speeds add: the intercept happens at roughly t ≈ Rslant / (v₀ + vt).
